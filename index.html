Sangram Prusty is a Ph.D. candidate in Theoretical and Computational Chemistry at Boston University, co-advised by Prof. Qiang Cui and Prof. John E. Straub. His research lies at the intersection of statistical mechanics, computational biophysics, and cell biology, driven by a fundamental question: how do complex molecular environments shape biomolecular interactions, dynamics, and biological function?

His doctoral research investigates the reciprocal relationship between membrane heterogeneity and protein behavior—how lipid composition and membrane physical properties influence protein conformational dynamics, association, and molecular recognition, and how proteins, in turn, reorganize their surrounding membranes. By combining multiscale molecular simulations, enhanced sampling approaches, and statistical mechanics with experimental insights, he connects molecular-level physical principles to collective membrane behavior and cellular function. His work has advanced our understanding of membrane phase-separation dynamics, protein association relevant to Alzheimer's disease, and membrane-dependent intracellular protein trafficking, while contributing to the development and validation of molecular force fields. Building on these studies, his long-term research vision is to integrate statistical mechanics, molecular dynamics simulations, machine learning, and experimental insights to develop predictive models of biomolecular processes in heterogeneous and crowded cellular environments.

Before joining Boston University, Sangram earned his BS–MS dual degree in Chemical Sciences, with a minor in Biological Sciences, from the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research Kolkata, India, supported by the DST–INSPIRE Scholarship, a competitive (top 1%) national merit-based award. His research has resulted in multiple first-author publications in leading journals of theoretical and computational chemistry and collaborations with NMR spectroscopists, cell biologists, and force-field developers. Beyond research, he mentors undergraduate and high-school students through Boston University's UROP and RISE programs, contributes to teaching in physical chemistry and scientific computing, and helps foster interdisciplinary scientific exchange through the Greater Boston Area TheoChem Seminar Series.

Research

My research bridges physical chemistry, machine learning, and biology, integrating statistical mechanics, molecular simulations, and experimental insights to understand how molecular interactions and complex environments govern biomolecular dynamics, organization, and function.

Membrane Heterogeneity: Emergence, Thermodynamics, Dynamics, and Biological Function

Biological membranes are dynamic, heterogeneous environments whose lateral organization emerges from collective lipid interactions and evolves through processes such as phase separation. My research investigates how microscopic molecular interactions govern the emergence and kinetics of membrane heterogeneity, and how the resulting variations in lipid composition and membrane physical properties influence protein conformational dynamics, association, and cellular function.

Using atomistic and coarse-grained molecular simulations combined with statistical mechanical approaches, I investigate the kinetics and mechanisms of lipid phase separation, identifying common domain-growth behavior across molecular resolutions despite differences in their microscopic interactions and dynamical timescales. Building on these insights, I use enhanced sampling and free-energy calculations to quantify how membrane heterogeneity regulates the thermodynamic competition between APP C99 dimerization and partitioning into liquid-ordered and liquid-disordered domains. In collaboration with experimental cell biologists, I also investigate how bilayer thickness and transmembrane-domain dynamics influence cargo recognition by Erv14/cornichon, connecting molecular mechanisms to directional ER–Golgi trafficking.

Force-field development: polarizable and coarse-grained models

The predictive accuracy of molecular simulations depends on how molecular interactions are represented across different levels of resolution. My research focuses on developing and evaluating molecular force fields to understand how their underlying physical approximations influence biomolecular conformational ensembles, dynamics, and thermodynamics.

At the atomistic level, I systematically compared polarizable Drude and fixed-charge CHARMM force fields, evaluating their ability to reproduce protein conformational ensembles against NMR measurements in collaboration with Prof. Rafael Brüschweiler. This work examined the roles of electronic polarization, protein conformation, and solvation in determining simulation accuracy. At the coarse-grained level, I contribute to the development and validation of Martini protein force fields in collaboration with Prof. Siewert-Jan Marrink, Dr. Paulo C. T. Souza, and colleagues, with the goal of improving transferable molecular models for biomolecular simulations.

Fluctuations and allostery in RNA–protein recognition

Before my Ph.D., I studied how the HIV Tat peptide finds the TAR RNA. Using molecular dynamics, enhanced sampling and non-affine fluctuation analysis, a concept borrowed from the physics of disordered solids, I showed that correlated domain fluctuations navigate the peptide's search along RNA.

This line of work also contributed to understanding how RNA uses dynamic conformational selection and entropic allostery to control cascades of protein binding.

Research Vision

I aim to build predictive, physics-based molecular models that integrate statistical mechanics, multiscale simulations, machine learning, and rare-event sampling with experimental insights to uncover how heterogeneous and crowded cellular environments govern biomolecular thermodynamics, dynamics, and collective behavior, ultimately bridging molecular-level physical mechanisms with cellular function and advancing our understanding of processes underlying human health and disease.
